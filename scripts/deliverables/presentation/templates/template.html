Method

How we read a recommendation

A specialist is a valid option if they are

  • in the same specialty as the specialist chosen
  • active (a claim in the past year)
  • not already seen by the patient in this specialty (first-time referrals; the specialist actually chosen is always included)
  • within 25 miles of the patient

Referrals analysed

  • First-time referrals to specialties where the PCP picks a named doctor
  • Facility services (radiology, pathology, emergency, anesthesiology) and generic labels (internal medicine, NP, PA) left out
  • referrals in 2018, in 2019

MIPS clean-up

  • Score 0 in the data = “no MIPS score” → specialty average, flagged
  • Several scores (one per practice) → their average
  • Scores bunch at the top: median , score exactly 100

Two estimates of quality gain

  • Standard: each specialist at their average score
  • Cautious: recommended at their lowest score, no-score = 0, chosen specialist at their highest

Inside the model

What each input does to the recommendations

Each bar: the model’s ranking with one input switched off, across all 2019 (or 2018) referrals. The model itself is unchanged.

Without the PCP’s history, recommendations shift to specialists new to the PCP, away from the busiest ones, and closer to the patient: that input is what ties recommendations to familiar, busy specialists. Switching off MIPS changes almost nothing (the model does not use it).

Steering

Quality vs fit: the main trade-off

Giving MIPS more weight moves recommendations to higher-quality specialists at some cost in fit. The graph model gives better trade-offs than the PCP’s own habit.
